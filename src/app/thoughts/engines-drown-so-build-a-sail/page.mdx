For months, Cursor was just another wrapper on top of GPT-4—sluggish, unreliable, easy to dismiss. Then Claude 3.5 Sonnet arrived. Same product, same codebase, and suddenly they're doing $100M ARR and every developer I know won't shut up about it.

They didn't build a product so much as a sail: a simple structure positioned to catch capability the moment it arrived.

## Engines and sails

That's the distinction I think separates the AI companies that will survive from the ones that won't.

**Engines** are complex architectures optimized for today's models. They're impressive now and worthless when the models improve.

**Sails** are simple architectures that get more powerful as models improve. They barely work today and dominate tomorrow.

Most founders are building engines. Think about what fine-tuning actually is: spending thousands of dollars teaching GPT-4 to be slightly less bad at your specific use case—an elaborate workaround for today's limitations. It's spending 2007 perfecting your mobile-optimized website while the iPhone is coming. Every dollar spent on fine-tuning is a dollar bet against model improvements.

## I built tool use before it was cool

In 2022, I was building Pluto. We watched GPT-3 try to analyze financial data and fail: it couldn't do math, couldn't track multiple variables, hallucinated spreadsheet functions. The standard response was elaborate prompt chains, token-optimized templates, multi-stage validation pipelines.

I built something simpler—I just let the model call functions. About 500 lines of code that said: here are tools, use them. This was a full year before function calling showed up in any commercial API.

GPT-3 mostly couldn't figure it out. Our demos were painful, and investors passed—why build features for capabilities that don't exist? But when GPT-4 arrived, and more importantly when it learned to use tools, the same architecture worked with no changes. Eighteen months after those painful demos, Robinhood acquired Pluto, and that architecture was a big part of why.

I didn't predict GPT-4's features. I built infrastructure for how humans naturally think—"I need to check this data, run this calculation, compare these things"—and waited for a model that could drive it.

## Complexity is a bet against progress

Look at any AI startup's architecture. If it would have been impressive in 2023, it's in trouble.

RAG pipelines are a monument to 8K context windows; frontier models are heading toward million-token context. Vector databases are infrastructure for models that can't remember; as context and retrieval-by-attention improve, that need shrinks. Heavy multi-agent orchestration is a workaround for models that can't yet hold a complex task; each model generation absorbs more of it.

These aren't features. They're monuments to the current model's failures. Every major model release re-sorts the industry: architectures built around the old limitations lose their reason to exist, and the simple ones inherit the new capability for free.

Prompt engineering follows the same curve. It's not that the people are bad—it's that the job is a workaround, and workarounds have a half-life. "Prompt engineer" will sound like "webmaster." In 2005 every company needed one; in 2010 every 12-year-old could build a website.

## The workflow extinction event

Here's the pattern underneath all of this:

Human → Human (how work used to happen)  
Human → AI → AI → Human (how work happens now)  
AI → AI (how work will happen)

If your product helps humans create things for other humans *in a business context*, you're building for a shrinking use case. Landing page builders, business document tools, sales email software—these all assume a human on both ends of the transaction. The entire B2B SaaS industry is predicated on humans needing interfaces. AIs don't need interfaces. They need APIs.

## The $4.2M signal

A16Z published data showing consumer AI companies hit $4.2M ARR in their first year while B2B companies hit $2M, and everyone acted confused about consumer beating enterprise.

But look closer at those "consumer" companies: FaceTune for aspiring influencers, AI writers for newsletter hustlers, headshot apps for LinkedIn. That's not consumer—it's prosumer. People trying to make money will pay $30/month for any edge.

What it proves is that humans will pay for AI leverage. And when people pay $30/month to work, imagine what they'll pay to play. The real consumer platforms—the ones that capture pure human creative expression—haven't arrived yet. We're in 1999: people can build personal websites, but YouTube doesn't exist.

## Only two things survive

In five years, I think only two types of AI companies exist:

1. **Infrastructure for AI-to-AI communication.** Boring but necessary—and probably not built by humans for long. AIs will build their own protocols.

2. **Platforms for human creative expression.** Where humans use AI to create things for other humans to experience. Not because they have to, but because they want to.

"AI-assisted business workflows" is the unstable middle: either the AI does it completely, or humans do it for fun. We saw this shape with the internet—the tools (FrontPage, Dreamweaver) died, the platforms (YouTube, Instagram, TikTok) won. AI follows the same pattern, faster.

## The wrong optimization

The common failure mode: watch today's model fail at something, then build elaborate infrastructure to fix it. That's solving for a model that will be obsolete before your next board meeting. The founders who win don't build for what AI can't do—they build for what humans are trying to do.

A useful heuristic: when your product is embarrassingly simple, you might have built a sail. When you're proud of your technical complexity, you definitely built an engine.

In 18 months, everything models can do 80% well today, they'll do well enough to deploy. That last 20% is the difference between demo and deployment—and when it closes, every optimization for today's constraints becomes irrelevant. Every workaround worthless. Every moat drained.

Except sails. Sails just catch more wind.

Cursor built a simple container for AI coding capability and waited. At Pluto, I built simple tool use and waited. The pattern is the same: don't compete on compensating for the model. Build the simple thing, position it where the wind will be strongest, and let the models make it good.

Because when the water rises, the engines go down with it.
