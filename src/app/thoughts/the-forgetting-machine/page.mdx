Nothing in Minecraft tells you to punch the tree.

Nothing warns you that night is coming, or what the crafting table does, or that the green thing walking toward you is about to explode. The game was, for practical purposes, unplayable without another kid who already knew. Everything you needed lived outside the game, in the schoolyard, the computer lab, the wiki, the older brother, passed hand to hand like it was contraband. And it became the best-selling game in the history of the medium.

Hold that up against everything the games industry has learned in forty years of playtests, funnels, retention curves, and postmortems, and Minecraft is a catalog of errors. No tutorial. No goals. No onboarding. A brutal, unexplained first night. The most successful product the medium ever produced fails the medium's entire accumulated curriculum. There are two ways to resolve that. Either Minecraft is a miracle, or the curriculum is wrong, and I want to argue something stronger than the curriculum being wrong. The industry thinks it has been learning how to build products this whole time. It has been forgetting, and the learning machinery is the thing doing the forgetting.

## Forgetting at n=1

You can watch it happen inside a single studio, four years, one franchise.

The first thing you do in Viva Piñata is walk down a beautiful hill into a ruined garden and talk to a crying girl standing in the rubble. She hands you a shovel and asks for help cleaning up the mess. You start by beating cracked ground into dirt. Effort, almost violence. Then a worm appears, because the dirt you just made is its habitat. In one minute the game has taught its entire thesis without a single rail: life here is downstream of your work. The worm feels like a consequence, so it feels alive.

The sequel throws you into a garden that already has grass, flowers, and a creature in a fence. Another appears before you've done anything, and within seconds a character, reached by following prompts into a menu, assigns you a challenge. Same studio, same systems, opposite ontology. The first game tells you *this place will react to you; you are its causal force*. The sequel tells you *this place has content for you, and we'll show you where it is*. Your first verb in one is labor you chose; in the other, compliance inside a playpen.

Here's the important part: the sequel's changes weren't mistakes. They were improvements, by every measurement a studio knows how to take. The empty start playtested as friction. Players took too long to reach their first piñata. So the garden got pre-filled, the goals got assigned, and something load-bearing quietly died, because ownership comes from the world being provably your fault. Nobody on that team was lazy or greedy. The committee wasn't evil. It was blind in a specific direction, and the blindness has a mechanism.

## The filter

The industry does have a learning system: playtests, A/B tests, dashboards, postmortems, conference talks. The system has a property nobody chose. It can only encode what's legible.

Friction's costs are legible. Churn at minute twenty, funnel drop-off, a confused face in a lab. Friction's benefits are illegible: ownership, folklore, the story someone tells in ten years, the friend who teaches you the secret. None of that casts a statistical shadow inside a quarter. So every measurement-driven decision removes friction, and the door only swings one way, because no producer ever green-lights restoring confusion. That looks insane in the data. Each removal is locally correct, and the sum is a game nobody loves.

Then the filter compounds across generations. A designer's taste is trained inside the games they grew up on. The cohort that built the classics had intuitions forged where friction was unavoidable; they knew in their hands what an unexplained world does to a player, so they could overrule a playtest. A designer raised after the smoothing doesn't experience the tooltip layer as a choice. It's just what games are. And between broken-up teams, only the articulable transmits — systems, content cadence, retention tricks — while the tacit part dies in every handoff. Run that for four generations and you get photocopies of photocopies. What survives is exactly what fit through the instruments.

The proof is what happens when someone defects and wins. Demon's Souls ignored a decade of onboarding wisdom, and the industry could not absorb the actual finding. The mechanism was obscurity forcing communal sensemaking: the wiki, the ghost messages, the friend who knows. The learning system extracted "difficulty," the one measurable ingredient, and shipped ten years of hard games with quest markers. Even the counterexamples get lossy-compressed into the wrong lesson. It's not that nobody knows the old wisdom. It's that the industry structurally cannot remember it.

## No safe targets

The obvious defense is that we just picked bad metrics. Engagement was a proxy, retention was a proxy; pick honest numbers and the machine learns true things. So take the most honest number anyone has: latency. Not a proxy, barely gameable, universally loved. Nobody's dark pattern ever made a game faster.

Chasing it is how MMO worlds went static. NPC schedules, physics, persistent simulation — none of it fits the tick budget, and "computationally expensive" is nearly a synonym for "interesting." The worlds got faster and stopped being alive. The web hit its Core Web Vitals the same way, by becoming skeleton screens and deferred husks: the page paints in 800 milliseconds and contains nothing. The metric was real and honestly earned. The thing it silently priced at zero was the entire reason to be there.

That's the deeper defect, and it has nothing to do with proxies. A metric specifies what to achieve and is structurally silent about what may be sacrificed to achieve it. Optimization pours everything unpriced into the furnace. Which means the question was never which number to pick. It's what the number is allowed to do. A crash rate you glance at tells you something broke. A crash rate that rules means nobody ships anything risky enough to crash, which is everything good. Nintendo shipped Breath of the Wild with framerate drops nobody remembers; a studio where framerate was sovereign would have cut the physics that made it worth remembering. Same instrument, opposite products.

There are no safe targets. Only safe monitors.

There's a timing corollary I hold for products generally. Metrics are trailing indicators of feeling at scale. Once something is loved, the dashboard is a fine microscope for optimizing its delivery. But feeling has to exist before it can cast a shadow, and instruments pointed at a product pre-feeling don't measure nothing. They measure the legible fraction of noise, and then drag the product toward whatever that fraction rewards. Measurement is for scaling a known good, not discovering one. Discovery runs on taste, conviction, and watching five strangers' faces. The industry's disease is running the scaling toolkit during the discovery phase, permanently, at every studio at once.

I run a game platform, so none of this is spectator opinion. These are rules we live under, and they cost us regularly. Spawn ships mysteries we refuse to document, and our players build the folklore, including a confidently wrong method that circulated for a day before the community tested it. In playtests the house rule is to stay quiet and let people struggle, because directing them skips the pain, and the exercise exists to find the pain. Our own dashboard argues with me constantly, and it's not wrong about what it sees. It's wrong about what it can't.

## The machine that studies harder

The bleakest part isn't that the knowledge was lost. It's that more diligence makes it worse. Every additional playtest, every finer-grained funnel, every well-run postmortem pushes more decisions through the filter that strips the tacit part. A studio in 1995 knew it was guessing, and guessing kept taste in the loop. A studio today has dashboards proving it isn't guessing, which is precisely why it can't notice what fell out. The confidence is the forgetting.

And games are only the leading edge. They're the most feeling-dense product category on earth and the most instrumented, so the contradiction reached full strength there first. The same machine is running on everything that was ever supposed to make someone feel something. Games are just what the output looks like when it ships at sixty frames a second.

In 2010, Minecraft generated a small wooden house for you at spawn. Players called it the Indev House. It was the industry's answer, delivered before the industry could deliver it: shelter provided, first night solved, friction removed. Notch deleted it. One designer ran the forty-year arc in reverse, on instinct, against everything the data would have said, and the game without the house is the one the whole world remembers. The machine has been trying to build that house back ever since. It calls this learning.
