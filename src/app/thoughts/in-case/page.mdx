# Politely by the Door

The dream put us all back at my grandmother's house, and at some point the phone rang, except what came out of it was a color, not a sound. I want to say orange. It wasn't orange, it was one I'd never heard before, and yes, I know how that sounds. My grandmother keeps her teeth in a kitchen drawer that has "Tuesdays" written on it in ballpoint. She went and got them out and put them in and answered with them, and then she sat there listening for what felt like ten minutes. Then she hung up and told the rest of us the call hadn't been for anybody in the family. It was for the house.

And the walls blushed. I swear. I was standing right there in the hallway and I watched them go pink from the baseboards up.

Out the front window the mailman was going door to door delivering yesterday, and he had most of the addresses wrong. There was a woman at the corner trying to return her shadow, the way you'd return a coat that didn't fit, but the man at the counter kept asking for a receipt she didn't have, so the shadow stayed with her. Faced the wrong way for the rest of the summer, sulking I guess, but it stayed.

I asked my father what time it was. He said didn't I hear, the clocks held a vote and seceded, and from now on all they were going to measure was the distance between strangers. Which, honestly, is what they'd been doing all along.

In the kitchen the bread was remembering wheat and the butter was in the middle of forgetting France. I took a conversation I'd been saving since I was a kid and spread both of them on it and ate it, the whole thing, every word I should have said to you, and honestly what it tasted like was a room I was never allowed into. Or snow falling on a photograph of snow, if that had a sound. In the dream it did.

Then the phone went off again and this time the color was your name. I've never learned to say your name right, is the thing. Not even asleep. Not even on the nights when the vowels show up on time and the consonants just stand there by the door being polite about it, like guests who already know nobody's going to ask them in.

# The Key

My father's hands smelled like engine oil, and under that like the specific quiet two people get when they've agreed, without ever saying so, to call a wall a door. He wore his keys on his belt. For years I heard them jingling down the hall and thought that sound was safety. It wasn't. It was just keys.

One time he gave me one. Little copper thing, for the side entrance. "In case," he said, and then didn't finish. Never did. I put it in my pocket, and by the next morning the sentence had started growing in there. I could feel it coming up through the lining, warm, like a root looking for the rest of itself, and I started walking carefully, because if it ever reached me it was going to finish, and I wasn't ready to hear whatever came after in case.

The house was white. We stopped living in it on a Tuesday, don't ask me why I remember the day of the week and nothing else. When my father moved out he took his silence with him, except it went to pieces on him in the move and he didn't get all of it, and for years afterward I kept coming across the parts he missed. Behind the dryer once. Once in the phone book, a thin piece, pressed flat like my mother used to press flowers. I collected them in a jar and I'll tell you, some nights that jar moved.

I did go back, one time, years after the new people bought it. They'd painted the side door shut. I put my little copper key against the painted-over seam anyway, and it went in, and it turned. The door didn't open into the house though. This is the part nobody believes. It opened into the sentence, into whatever was supposed to come after in case. Inside was a little room with nothing in it but a chair somebody had set up facing the window, and when I looked out that window what I saw was mornings. His mornings. Every one where he walked out of the house without telling anybody where he was going, all of them going on at once out there, and believe me, that turned out to be a lot of mornings.

I sat down in the chair and felt the sentence let go of my wrist, and it died, quietly, which I understand now is just how finished things die.

I keep wanting to say the view was beautiful. It wasn't beautiful. It was early out there, over and over, and he was always in the middle of leaving, and the whole time I'd been carrying that key in my pocket, this was the room it opened.

So I sat in it a while.

# The Leak Is Proof

There's a shop on Edmond Street that repairs questions people have dropped, so I brought mine in. Mine had a hairline crack running right through the load-bearing clause. The mechanic said yeah, he sees that one all the time, that's your classic inherited-from-a-father question. He held it up under the fluorescents and showed me where all the stress had pooled, right at the hinge between *how does this work* and *will you show me*. He could fix the first half, he told me. The second half was structural, and if he went anywhere near it the whole thing would open up into a room, and he wasn't insured for rooms.

I said I'd take it as-is. He wrapped it up in a receipt from a conversation I had in 1996 and charged me the sound a keyring makes when the man wearing it thinks nobody's around.

I paid. That's one currency I've never run short of.

Out front, across the street, a woman was returning a set of answers to the store over there. Too precise, she was telling the clerk. They fit so well she couldn't feel them anymore. The clerk went in the back and came out with something in a looser tolerance, a half-answer, still warm, the kind that rattles a little when you carry it so at least you know it's there. She said her father had one just like it. They all did, the clerk said. That's the whole reason the store exists.

I took my cracked question home on the bus. The route only runs between *almost* and *too late* now. Used to go all the way out to *exactly right*, the driver told me, but they cut that part years ago because nobody ever recognized the stop.

By the time I got home the crack had spread. I could see into it if I held it up. Inside there was a workshop, a small one, lit the way things are lit after midnight when a man is explaining something he loves to a kid who's too young to follow it but not too young to feel what it means to be the one he picked to tell. Tools on the wall. They were hung in the shape of a sentence nobody ever finished.

I tried to climb in. The crack took my weight fine but it wouldn't take my age. Rated for a child and rated for a man and nothing in between, apparently. So I sat down outside it and listened. The tools in there hum. And the hum is at exactly the frequency of a man who knows more than he was ever built to hold, and I know that frequency, because I've been putting it out myself for years now, alone, at a workbench I don't remember building, tightening things that won't stay tight. The crack is where the sound gets out. I'm not going to fix it. That leak is the only proof I have that the room was real.
