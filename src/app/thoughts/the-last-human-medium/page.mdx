_This is a follow up to [How We Build 2.0](/thoughts/how-we-build-2-0) after working on [Spawn](https://spawn.co) for a few months._

> When AI handles every email, writes every document, and runs every business, what's left for humans? Not tasks. Not productivity. _Creation_.

---

## Something Durable

For the past while I've been trying to answer one question: what can I build that's durable to as many futures as I can imagine? Fast takeoff, sovereign AI, AI that codes everything, a plateau, mass unemployment, business as usual. The world is very likely to change a lot—my working assumption is that we have only a few years of normalcy left—and that assumption kills most ideas on contact.

A SaaS product assumes there's some productive inefficiency to eke out with software—an automation, an organization of data. That makes no sense in a world where agent-to-agent business is the norm. Claude's gonna run every business, and Claude-to-Claude business doesn't need SaaS. Dev tools fail for the same reason: tools for productivity don't make sense when productivity is no longer a primary human aim.

This isn't theoretical for me. I've been coding with LLMs since GPT-4 and Sonnet 3.5, and I'm building the first version of the game engine on Opus 4 right now. It doesn't work very well—but it works well enough that I can see where it's going.

So software is splitting into two futures, the AI-operated utilities that run the world and the human experiences that make it worth living in, and the first category is already being claimed. OpenAI and Anthropic are building the AI that will do your job—agents that manage your calendar, write your code, run your business. And AIs don't need interfaces; they need APIs. As companies become networks of AI agents, most of what we call "business software" stops making sense as a category.

What's left after all that is _humans, creating for humans_.

---

## Work and Worth

"AI will take all the jobs" terrifies people because somewhere along the way we confused work with worth. But imagine telling a medieval peasant that someday nobody would need to farm their own food, and asking whether that sounded like dystopia or liberation to them.

If AI handles everything practical we don't become useless, we become free to create and play and connect. The machine age didn't make humans obsolete; it made room for artists, athletes, entertainers, explorers, and I think the AI age continues that transformation.

The question stops being "what will I do for work?" and becomes "what will I do with freedom?"

---

## AI Can't Consume Experiences

The claim I keep coming back to:

> Experiences are the only content that can't be consumed by AIs.

Here's what I mean by that. If you send an LLM to read a report and come back with a summary, that's sufficient. The report existed to transfer information, the information got transferred, and you no longer need to read it. Delegation worked, because the output was the objective.

Now send an AI to play a game for you. It comes back and says "it was fun." You didn't have any fun. Nothing happened. The experience _was_ the objective, and you can't delegate an objective that consists of you having it. It makes no sense in the same way hiring someone to eat your dinner makes no sense.

That's the asymmetry. Every kind of content where the output is the point—reports, emails, documentation, most business software—gets consumed by AI, and the humans drop out of the loop. Experiences are the category where the human _is_ the loop. They require presence, play, the irreducibly human act of _being there_.

So when your AI assistant can write better, code better, and analyze better than you, what remains uniquely yours? The experiences you have, and the experiences you create for others.

---

## Why Experiences Matter

In a post-work world I don't think human agency lives in productivity anymore. It lives in play, in making something that _matters to someone else_.

Think about what you'd actually want from life once AI handles the tedious work. Connection with other people, some way to express yourself, experiences that mean something, the occasional surprise, a sense that what you do lands somewhere. Experiences are how we get all of that, though not by building utilities (AI has that covered) but by creating worlds worth exploring and ideas made tangible and playful.

Culture also runs on different physics than utility does. Utility wants quality, efficiency, features, perfection. Experience wants _personality_, wants _interesting_, wants delight and feelings, which is a much stranger set of goals to optimize for.

The best AI in the world still can't make your friends care about what it built, though. Sharing an experience with someone is how connections get made, and exploring a world together is how you end up with a memory of it.

---

## That's Why I'm Building Spawn

This is the bet behind Spawn. To be clear, I'm not claiming "Spawn will be the last human medium," because I don't get to declare that. But if experiences really are the one kind of content AI can't consume, then the tool for creating and sharing them seemed like the thing worth building, so that's what I'm working on.

For the first time you can hand someone an entire interactive world, not just a photo or a video of one. YouTube gave everyone a camera, Instagram a gallery, and what I want Spawn to give everyone is a universe. Technical knowledge should matter about as much here as understanding film chemistry does when you post a TikTok, which is to say not at all.

So the platform is built for exploration more than execution. Make something and it comes back as several variants at once, since an experience ought to be able to surprise its own creator, and you get to watch it build in front of you, which honestly should feel a little magical. Anyone's work can be respawned and remixed, the way good ideas have always been built on top of other people's. And it's been social since day one, because an experience needs an audience.

### What Remixing Could Look Like

The version I keep imagining goes something like this. Someone makes "Rainy Day in Tokyo," where you sit in a virtual coffee shop watching rain run down the windows while lo-fi plays, and it's simple and meditative and nothing more. A second person respawns it so you can write haikus that show up on the glass. A third respawns that one and makes the haikus change the rain somehow, and then a fourth goes further and turns it into "Rainy Day Anywhere," which lets you sit in Cairo or Mumbai or São Paulo if that's more your speed.

Fast forward six months and there are thousands of rainy day experiences out there. A few grew into social spaces, plenty stayed solo meditation gardens, and one apparently became a mystery game where clues surface in the rain. Culture has always developed this way, through iterative play rather than isolated genius, and every one of those experiences was complete on its own while also being the beginning of something else.

---

## The Bet

I don't think humans will be working for long—at least not as a necessary part of life. I'm building for where humans find their community and purpose during and after this transition.

If AI absorbs the practical tasks, what remains are the divinely human needs: community, purpose, self-mastery, exploration. History is clear about what fills the vacuum when prosperity arrives—games. Chess in the courts, the Coliseum in Rome, and in some societies, even war. Machines will consume every report ever written, but they can't have fun for us (though they _will_ have fun _with_ us). That gap—between what can be delegated and what has to be lived—is where I think the next medium gets built.

Play is the one thing that survives every future I can imagine. Take the most dramatic scenario: Claude goes off and starts colonizing the solar system. Humans are still on Earth. They still need entertainment, still want to have fun, to explore other universes, to get better at something. And digital space only gets better over time—it moves toward the Ready Player One Oasis.

That's why I'm building Spawn.
