Twice a week, sometimes more, I get this tightening in my stomach right before I tell someone a truth they need to hear. I've started to think that feeling is what _real kindness_ actually is. Their face falls. If you're lucky it lifts again with understanding. Sometimes it means going into a conversation knowing it could end a friendship today, on the theory that whatever comes out the other side might be deeper.

---

At some point we all started using _nice_ and _kind_ like they were the same word. They aren't. Nice feels good right now. Kind mostly doesn't pay off until way down the line, and at a startup, where everything is compressed, mixing the two up can actually kill you.

I grew up professionally at Bridgewater, the hedge fund famous for its culture of brutal honesty, where people would tell you to your face that your ideas were terrible and why. So when I left to start my own company, basically everyone warned me I'd need to soften up. Be more "founder-friendly." Build a "positive culture." And look, _every day_ I'm tempted, because these conversations are brutal. I've watched friendships fracture over them. I've seen people walk out. I've lain awake at 2am wondering if I should have just kept my mouth shut.

But then, usually about two years later, I get the text:

> "Hey, I've been thinking about that conversation we had. Thank you."

The gap between impact and gratitude gets measured in _years_, which is the actual math of real kindness: you pay the price today for a thank you that might never arrive, and you watch relationships end hoping time turns them into something deeper.

---

## What It Feels Like

Uncomfortable, mostly. You finally have the conversation you've been putting off for two weeks and you notice your own voice getting quieter as the stakes go up. Being nice feels more like relief. You dodged a bullet, you kept the peace. Except you didn't really keep anything, you borrowed that relief from the future at a pretty brutal interest rate.

I used to assume emotional intelligence meant reading the room, smoothing things over, keeping everyone comfortable. Then I built a startup and found out comfort is a luxury a small team can't really afford. Kind sounds like "that's not going to work, and here's why." Nice sounds like "interesting approach!" followed by silence.

---

## What Being Nice Costs

I've fired people I'd avoided giving feedback to. That isn't leadership so much as theft, because I stole their chance to improve, and no amount of "being supportive" in the meantime made up for it.

Something I've noticed: the highest-stakes conversations happen in the smallest voices. You lean in. You speak carefully but clearly. You watch their eyes to make sure they're _really_ hearing you and not just waiting for it to end.

My co-founder and I have a rule that if something bothers you for more than 24 hours you have to say it out loud. The first few times it felt like defusing a bomb. Now it's more like breathing.

---

## The Long Game

I try to optimize for being respected in ten years rather than liked today, because whether someone appreciates me when they look back on their career matters more to me than whether they enjoy this particular Tuesday's standup.

No one looks back on their career and says "I'm so glad my first boss never gave me hard feedback." What they say is "that person who told me the truth when I needed to hear it changed my life." Being that person, though, sucks _in the moment_, every single time.

> The kindest thing you can do for someone who's betting years of their life on your shared mission is to be unflinchingly honest with them.

They didn't join your startup for comfort, they joined to build something real, so treat them like they can handle _reality_.

---

## The Paradox

It would be so much easier to be nice, to dodge and smooth over and be the founder everyone likes working for today, but I've seen what happens to teams built on polite fictions: they move slowly right up until they die quickly.

The paradox is that teams built on the kind of kindness that doesn't feel nice actually _like each other more_ in the long run. Once you know someone will tell you the truth, you can finally relax, stop performing, stop wondering what they really think, and just _build_.

Nice is optimizing for comfort in the moment, whereas being kind means playing for growth and trust over years. Choose kind, especially when it doesn't feel nice.
